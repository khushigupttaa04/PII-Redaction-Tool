\section{How I built the ground truth}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Stratified sampling with fixed seeds.} From each document I drew PII-dense paragraphs (chosen by a keyword filter independent of the tool), random short paragraphs (table cells, headings) to catch false positives, and long prose where NER mistakes are most likely.
\item \textbf{One labelling rule-book} for every set (Table~1 of the README): people, contact details, addresses, IDs, private companies and their brands are PII; regulators, legal terms, prices, filing dates and order/ticket numbers are not.
\item \textbf{Dev/test split.} I tuned only on the dev set. The test set and the 5 unseen prospectuses were never used to change the code.
\item \textbf{Synthetic and injected data} have labels by construction, because I wrote or planted every value.
\end{itemize}

\section{Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{True positive (TP):} a prediction with the same PII type as a labelled entity whose text overlaps it. \textbf{FP:} a prediction matching nothing. \textbf{FN:} a labelled entity that was missed.
\item \textbf{Precision} = TP/(TP+FP): how much of what I redacted was really PII. \textbf{Recall} = TP/(TP+FN): how much of the real PII I caught. \textbf{F1} = their harmonic mean. All are micro-averaged over types.
\item \textbf{Redaction-level} scores ignore the type: they ask only whether the PII was replaced, which is what matters for privacy.
\item \textbf{Accuracy} has no natural definition for span detection, so I report \emph{token-level accuracy}: the share of words correctly marked PII / not PII. It is inflated because most words are not PII (redacting nothing would score 88.0\% on the test set), so precision and recall are the numbers to read.
\end{itemize}

\section{How the Anjali Labtech prospectus shaped the rules}
Before the benchmark, I ran the tool on one extra prospectus (Anjali Labtech, a PDF converted to Word) and compared the output with a list of every identifier visible in it. Its leaks showed failure modes the Red Herring Prospectus never exposed, and each became a \emph{general} rule rather than a document-specific fix.

\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}LLP{1.6cm}P{1.6cm}@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Failure found} & \textbf{General rule added} & \textbf{Before} & \textbf{After} \\
\midrule
Partial names left beside fakes; two promoters merged into one & Split name runs at ordinary words (``AND''); grow partial names over neighbouring name words & 20/26 names & 26/26 names \\
Emails and links broken across lines by PDF conversion & Match emails and links with a stray space or line break & 2/5 & 5/5 \\
Foreign company forms (``DMCC'', ``GmbH'') & Legal-entity suffixes for 100+ countries (\texttt{cleanco}) & 2 missed & 0 missed \\
Auditor's firm registration number & Any ID written after a label (FRN, registration no.) & missed & removed \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}\\[2pt]
{\small Legal terms stayed untouched throughout, and the Red Herring test set did not regress. Because these rules were written after seeing Anjali's errors, its ``after'' column is not a held-out result; the 5-prospectus benchmark is.}

\section{Limitations of this evaluation}
\begin{itemize}
\item One annotator (me); a second annotator and inter-annotator agreement would make the labels more reliable.
\item Small samples: 65 test entities and 42 benchmark entities, so per-type and per-document numbers have wide confidence intervals.
\item I wrote both the synthetic ticket log and the patterns, so ticket-log recall is likely optimistic; the injection test partly offsets this with random values in real text.
\item Dates and financial figures are kept for usefulness; combined with the industry and offer size they could let someone re-identify the issuer through public SEBI filings.
\item The benchmark documents were converted from PDF, so conversion quality affects the scores as much as detection does.
\end{itemize}
